# Auxiliary Nodes for BP Decoding of Quantum LDPC Codes 

Daniel Tandler, Paul Bezner, and Stephan ten Brink 

Institute of Telecommunications, University of Stuttgart, Pfaffenwaldring 47, 70569 Stuttgart, Germany 

{tandler, bezner, tenbrink }@inue.uni-stuttgart.de 

Abstract —Many recently proposed Calderbank–Shor–Steane (CSS) quantum low-density parity-check (QLDPC) codes have sparse decoding graphs, enabling syndrome-based belief prop-agation (BP) decoding at low complexity. Their construction, however, often results in properties that impair BP performance, such as short cycles and degeneracy. In this work, we propose a general framework for introducing auxiliary variable nodes (AVNs) and auxiliary check nodes (ACNs) into the decoding graph of CSS codes, compatible with the standard stabilizer measurement framework. This provides an additional degree of freedom in the design of the decoding graph itself and can be used to tackle the aforementioned shortcomings. We show that recently proposed techniques, 4-cycle removal and subcode ensemble decoding, can be interpreted as instances of this framework. For 

4-cycle removal, we find that the gains depend strongly on the BP iteration count and check-node message scaling. Building on this framework, we further propose a graph-derived subcode ensemble decoder and demonstrate under circuit-level noise that it substantially reduces the per-round logical error rate compared with BP on the corresponding 4-cycle-free decoding graph. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Belief propagation (BP) decoding is attractive for quan-tum low-density parity-check (QLDPC) codes due to its low complexity and direct compatibility with sparse parity-check representations. However, the performance of BP decoding is highly sensitive to the chosen Tanner-graph representation. In particular, trapping sets, often consisting of short cycles ,and degeneracy -induced symmetries can strongly degrade the decoding performance. Short cycles in the Tanner graph cause the messages in BP to become strongly correlated and thus oscillate and reinforce potential wrong beliefs. Degeneracy refers to the phenomenon that can occur in syndrome based decoding, where multiple error hypotheses can share the same syndrome. While in theory finding a single one of these hypotheses can be sufficient in the quantum setting, this ambiguity can strongly impair the BP decoder’s performance. This is especially strong in QLDPC codes as they tend to have low-weight stabilizers. One line of research aims to circumvent these issues by applying different decoding methods, usually after a first pass of BP decoding [1], [2], [3]. While improving performance, this can also increase the worst-case latency, which might be problematic for practical quantum error correction (QEC). Other approaches [4], [5], [6], [7], aim to introduce decoding diversity by changing the decoding trajectory that BP sees during different decoding runs. A. System Model 

In this work, we consider QEC using CSS stabilizer codes. A CSS code can be described by a pair of binary matrices HX ,corresponding to X-type stabilizers, and HZ , corresponding to Z-type stabilizers. Error detection and correction can be performed by measuring the stabilizers and using the binary measurement results to infer the error location. In practice this syndrome extraction is realized using a sequence of quantum gates, resulting in a measurement circuit . As this process can be noisy itself, it can introduce additional errors. This work adopts the widely recognized circuit-level noise model 

(together with the corresponding DEM) for Quantum Error Correction as described in e.g. [16], [17], [18], which can also model these errors. Here, it is assumed that independent errors can occur at any position in the syndrome extraction circuit (state preparation, measurement, single- and two-qubit gates). We assume an equal probability p for each error class. B. Syndrome-Based Belief Propagation (BP) Decoding 

Similar to their classical counterparts, QLDPC codes can be decoded using a variant of the message passing BP decoder operating on the decoding graph of the code, derived from the DEM. The decoder iteratively exchanges scalar messages between the graph’s VNs and check nodes (CNs) and outputs an estimate of the marginal probability of error for each VN. Decoding is finished if a maximum number of iterations I is reached. The initial information for the decoder is given by the measured syndrome vector s and the initial channel statistics. A. Direct graph modification 

The GARI method proposed in [13] introduces auxiliary nodes to eliminate the 4-cycles that the Y -component of the decoding matrices induces in the DEM. Although the underly-ing graph transformation is stated for general decoding graphs, its application in [13] is restricted to the 4-cycles arising from Y -type error mechanisms. Each such augmentation is an instance of steps (1)–(3) of our framework: the vector c′ is supported on the columns of the targeted Y -induced biclique, and the corresponding row additions follow. The resulting augmentation thus takes the form of Eq. 9, and Proposition 1 recovers the decoding-problem equivalence established in [13]. Although [13] remarks that the transformation can be applied recursively to the remaining 4-cycles, this option is not ex-plored in its numerical evaluation. In contrast, we apply the optimized AVN/ACN-based 4-cycle-removal strategy of [12] to the X-type decoding matrix. The algorithm of [12] greedily selects all-ones blocks containing δ 4-cycles each and removes them either by a plain row addition, if this is possible without creating degree-1 or degree-2 check nodes (Cases 1 and 2 of [12]), or by an AVN/ACN insertion.

B. Parity check matrix construction 

The 4-cycle removal algorithm was recently used in the con-struction of parity check matrices optimized for BP decoding of short classical channel codes in [12], [20], demonstrating significant gains in performance for some codes. For the quantum setting, [8] observed that adding redundant checks to the parity check matrix of CSS codes in the code ca-pacity model can improve performance of some codes while worsening that of others. In preliminary experiments on the codes considered here, straightforward adaptations of these constructions to the DEM parity check matrix did not yield consistent improvements over the standard matrix within our BP configuration; a systematic study, including tuning of the construction parameters, is left for future work. V. A FFINE SUBCODE VIEW 

Affine subcode ensemble decoding was introduced for short classical binary codes [14] and recently extended to the quan-tum setting [15]. The core idea is to append additional, linearly independent rows ( splitters ) to the parity check matrix of a CSS code in order to break degeneracy-induced symmetries that impair syndrome-based BP decoding. Since the syndrome bits of these added checks are not available before decoding, an ensemble of decoders is run, one for each hypothesized value. This can be viewed as the check-splitting postprocessing of [10] moved before BP, with the added benefit that it can also improve the structure of the decoding graph itself. This construction emerges naturally from our framework. By Lemma 1, fixing the AVN to a hypothesized value e′ = b′

restricts the original solution space E(H, s) to the affine constraint c′⊤ e = s′ + b′. The two hypotheses b′ ∈ { 0, 1}

partition the solution space, so exactly one branch of the resulting two-decoder ensemble contains the true error. Since elementary row additions leave the solution space unchanged, the same holds for the general construction of Eq. 9. Each step μ (0 ≤ μ < P ) of the 4-cycle removal process can be seen as appending a new row c⊤ 

> aug ,μ

to the matrix and then adding it onto existing rows. By Lemma 3, the appended row is a candidate splitter for subcode ensemble decoding [14] whenever its c′⊤ part lies outside Row( HDEM ) + Row( L).As simulations suggest, nearly all of these rows fulfill this criterion. We therefore propose to reuse the rows generated by 

4-cycle removal as splitter candidates for a subcode ensemble decoder. Since this yields far more candidates than can be used (P ), a selection rule is required; following the observation in [15], we further restrict each ensemble member to two decoding paths ( ∆ = 2 ). We propose two greedy selection strategies:  

> •

Static : The ensemble is built iteratively. The first member is the candidate with the lowest empirical logical error rate pL. Each subsequent member is the candidate that corrects the largest number of sampled error events on which the current ensemble fails (the maximum-coverage heuristic of [21]).  

> •

Adaptive : Standard BP is run for T iterations on the 4-cycle-free matrix. If the decoder did not converge to a syndrome-valid solution, the AVNs are ranked by their LLR magnitudes, and the ensemble is formed from the ACNs associated with the L least reliable AVNs. If multiple ensemble members produce syndrome-valid esti-mates, the minimum-Hamming-weight estimate is selected as the final correction. A. 4−cycle removal 

We first evaluate the effect of 4-cycle removal on the number of edges in the decoding graph, the 4-cycles and the error rate pL for various iteration counts I and check-node scaling factors α during fixed steps of the removal process. To this end, we first run a complete 4-cycle removal to determine the total number of removal steps. Then, we evenly divide this into 

15 intermediate checkpoints where we evaluate the previously mentioned quantities on the partially 4-cycle reduced matrices. The results are shown in Fig. 2. As expected, the number of 4-cycles steadily decreases during the removal process, with the first removal step removing the largest number of cycles. This is also reflected in the edge count of the matrices: The minimum does not lie at zero 4-cycles but arises shortly after the start of the removal process. Note that removing single 4-cycles with the ACN and AVN construction can indeed increase the number of edges in the matrix as 4 edges are removed and 5 edges introduced. This reveals a tradeoff between decoding complexity (in terms of edge count) and decoding performance. Most interesting is the pL behaviour over the removal process. First, note that the performance of the 4-cycle reduced augmented matrices generally improves for a check-node scaling α = 1 and a reasonable number of BP iterations. Remarkably, the final 4-cycle free matrices exhibit a strong dependence on α in their performance. For a small number of BP iterations, the decoding performance of the augmented graph worsens as more AVNs are introduced. One possible explanation is that the zero-prior-LLR AVNs provide no intrinsic information while simultaneously enlarging the decoding graph. Consequently, additional BP iterations may be required for useful information to propagate through the augmented graph. Furthermore, for a large iteration count, the original HDEM without any removed 4-cycles shows a performance nearly as good as the final 4-cycle removed one. 5

In this work, we introduced a general framework that incor-porates AVNs and ACNs into the decoding graph for binary syndrome-based decoding of QLDPC codes while preserving the original decoding problem. The framework provides a common algebraic interpretation of graph augmentation, 4-cycle removal and affine subcode constraints. We showed that while 4-cycle removal can improve the performance, its benefits strongly depend on the BP iteration count and check-node message scaling. By reusing the auxiliary nodes generated during 4-cycle removal as candidate degeneracy splitters, the proposed static and adaptive subcode ensembles substantially improve plain BP decoding. Improved decoding-matrix construction methods utilizing auxiliary nodes might further improve performance. Combining these methods with windowed decoding could be particularly effective for larger codes, as it could reduce the effective decoding problem size while retaining the benefits of graph-derived subcode ensembles.

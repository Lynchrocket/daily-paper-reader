# Row-Boosted Ensemble Belief Propagation for Short LDPC Codes 

Paul Bezner †, Felix Krieg †, and Stephan ten Brink 

Institute of Telecommunications, University of Stuttgart, Germany 

{bezner, krieg, tenbrink }@inue.uni-stuttgart.de 

Abstract —Belief-propagation (BP) decoding of short and moderate-length low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes is lim-ited by finite-length graph effects: a single decoder trajectory can become trapped or oscillatory even when an alternative trajectory would decode the received word. Existing ensemble-BP decoders create the required diversity through multiple parity-check matrices, automorphisms, modified schedules, subcodes, or altered update rules. We introduce row-boosted ensemble (RBE) decoding as a minimal decoder-side diversity mechanism: all ensemble members share the same parity-check matrix and the same BP kernel, and differ only in a small set of parity-check rows whose outgoing messages are boosted. On the 5G NR BG1 (144 , 96) code, RBE with 32 members lowers the frame-error rate of BP with 20 iterations (BP-20) from 1.6 × 10 −2

to 1.6 × 10 −3 at Eb/N 0 = 4 .0 dB , outperforming saturated-min-sum and affine subcode ensembles of equal size. Increasing the ensemble size yields additional gains, indicating that RBE provides a scalable performance–complexity tradeoff. The gains transfer across 5G NR block lengths and rates, to non-5G short LDPC codes, and across flooding and layered schedules. 

Index Terms —LDPC codes, belief propagation, ensemble de-coding, row boosting, short block codes 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes are decoded effi-ciently by sparse-graph message passing and are part of mod-ern communication standards. At short and moderate block lengths, however, a single belief propagation (BP) decoder remains far from maximum likelihood (ML) performance. Failures are dominated by the interaction between the received word and finite-length graph structures: short cycles, trapping and absorbing sets, and oscillatory message trajectories [1], [2]. Raising the iteration limit helps only when the underlying trajectory eventually escapes on its own; a single decoder has no way to handle error patterns to which it is inherently unsuited – it lacks decoder diversity .Ensemble-BP decoding removes this limitation by running several related decoders in parallel and selecting a valid candidate according to the channel metric. Diversity can be obtained from multiple parity-check bases (multiple-bases belief propagation (MBBP)) [3], [4], code automorphisms or broken-graph transforms [5], [6], affine subcodes [7],

These mechanisms are effective, but each requires an additional ingredient – such as a second parity-check representation or a non-absorbed code symmetry – that is not always available [10]. This paper introduces row-boosted ensemble (RBE)-BP de-coding as a code-agnostic ensemble method (Fig. 1). Each en-semble member uses the original parity-check matrix (PCM) and the same BP kernel, but boosts the outgoing messages of a different small subset of parity-check rows. RBE decoding thereby alters the iterative trajectory while preserving the code and the Tanner-graph storage, and can be added to any sum-product algorithm (SPA), min-sum algorithm (MSA), normalized min-sum algorithm (NMSA), flooding, or layered implementation. RBE is related to weighted and neural BP, which generalize the message-passing update with trainable edge or iteration weights [11]. However, such approaches typically optimize a single weighted decoder and store fine-grained weights across many edges and iterations, whereas RBE forms an ensemble from simple row-wise boosts of the original PCM. As BP decoders do not always converge to a valid codeword, post-processing (e.g., ordered-statistics decoding (OSD) [13] or systematic re-encoding) can ensure that all ensemble members produce valid codewords. This optional post-processing leads to non-empty lists L and typically increases the decoding performance of the ensemble. Throughout, BP-I denotes a plain BP decoder with Imax =

I; ensembles are labeled by their type and size, e.g., RBE-E32 for an RBE with E = 32 members. Concretely, MBBP builds an ensemble of algebraically equivalent parity-check matrices, so that cycles and trapping sets tied to one representation are not present in another [3], [4]. Automorphism ensemble decoding instead uses code au-tomorphisms to build an ensemble. For codes with a known symmetry (e.g., the cyclic shifts of a cyclic code), each mem-ber decodes a permuted copy of Lch and maps the result back through the inverse permutation, also leading to a potentially favorable noise representation in some ensemble members [5], [14]. In case BP is invariant to known automorphisms, the Tanner-graph symmetry can be broken in order to benefit from those automorphisms [6]. Affine-subcode ensembles add a small set of additional linear constraints to each member’s parity-check matrix. Each member decodes a subcode Ce whose graph may be better conditioned than that of C itself [7]. Saturated min-sum clips the magnitude of the least reliable message bits and enumerates all possible bit combinations in those positions. Each ensemble member corresponds to one possible bit sequence [15]. Schedule-diversity ensembles vary only the check-to-variable update order – i.e., the order in which the decoder schedules the layered updates [10]. III. R OW -B OOSTED ENSEMBLE DECODING

B. Decoding Complexity 

To reduce the decoding complexity of the ensemble, a gate decoder can check whether the full ensemble is required [15]. A plain BP decoder runs on the unmodified Tanner graph, and the boosted members are only evaluated when the gate produces no valid codeword. The final output is the ML-in-list candidate among the ensemble members. This keeps the average decoding complexity close to that of one BP decode, since most frames terminate in the gate. In rare cases where the gate converges on a wrong codeword, the ensemble loses the opportunity to “rescue” the frame. Therefore, the average complexity of a gated ensemble version is lower, but the FER is slightly increased. RBE adds only a row-boost table to an existing BP decoder, thus the average decoding complexity per member is approximately equal to that of a plain BP decoder, especially if NMSA is used.

D. Offline Row Selection

A. Comparison to Other Ensemble Methods 

Fig. 5 shows the FER on the 5G NR BG1 (144 , 96) code. Over the entire simulated signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) range, RBE-E32 clearly improves on plain BP-20 by roughly an order of magnitude at moderate-to-high SNR, and also out-performs long-iteration BP-640: additional diverse trajectories are more valuable than a longer single one. At the same ensemble size E = 32 , RBE achieves the lowest FER among all considered ensembles, outperforming the affine subcode ensemble [7], the saturated-min-sum en-semble [15], and the MBBP ensemble [3], [4] with 5 low weight checks appended to the original PCM. Broken-graph AED is limited to E = 5 members by the lifting structure of the code and, accordingly, provides only a small gain over plain BP-20. The residual gap to the ML reference is dominated by frames whose correct codeword is absent from the candidate list; mis-selections among valid candidates, including the rare event that the gate converges to a wrong codeword, account for only a small fraction of the errors.

RBE preserves the parity-check matrix, the Tanner-graph storage, the decoder kernel, the syndrome validation, and the candidate metric, and creates diversity solely by boosting selected rows in different ensemble members. The gains transfer across 5G NR block lengths and rates as well as to other code families with the greedy row search. We found that boosted rows reliably anchor failed BP trajectories, especially if their support is error-free. Notably, RBE does not rely on a highly tuned boost design. Even random row-boost configurations provide measurable gains and transfer across different scenarios. RBE is a low-overhead mechanism for decoder-side di-versity, not an ML decoder. It helps when changing the relative strength of selected parity checks redirects a failed BP trajectory toward the transmitted codeword; it is least effective when all boosted trajectories fail or when the received word is closer to a wrong valid codeword. The residual gap to ML is therefore a list-completeness limit: at high SNR, the correct codeword is predominantly absent from L. Since the selected rows transfer across the rate family without re-tuning and the useful boost factor spans a broad range, the design effort remains small.

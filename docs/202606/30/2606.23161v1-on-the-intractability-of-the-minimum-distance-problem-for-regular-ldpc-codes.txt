# On the Intractability of the Minimum Distance Problem for Regular LDPC Codes 

Chenyuan Jia, Qingqing Peng, Ke Liu, Guanghui Wang, Guiying Yan 

Abstract —The minimum distance problem (MDP) for low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes is a central problem in coding theory and is closely related to the analysis of low-weight codewords and error-floor behavior. Although the unrestricted MDP is computationally intractable, its complexity under degree constraints that commonly occur in LDPC code design has remained less clear. In this paper, we study the MDP for left regular and biregular Tanner graphs. We prove that the problem is NP -complete and W[1] -complete for J-left regular Tanner graphs for every fixed J ≥ 3, and also for (3 , 3) -regular bipartite graphs. We further establish W[1] -completeness for (J, K)-regular instances for every fixed J, K ≥ 3. The reductions are based on a degree-preserving transformation framework consisting of hyperedge decomposition, check node splitting, and controlled variable replication. These transformations transfer hardness between di ff erent degree distributions while preserving explicit bijections among nonzero codewords, even covers, and nonempty 

(a, 0) -trapping sets. The results delineate the computational limits of exact LDPC code analysis under natural regularity constraints. 

Index Terms —Regular LDPC codes, minimum distance, NP-completeness, W[1]-completeness. 

I. I ntroduction 

# LOW-DENSITY parity-check (LDPC) codes [1] are fun-damental in modern communication systems because they achieve capacity-approaching performance under belief-propagation (BP) decoding. Their high-SNR performance, however, may be limited by the error-floor phenomenon, where the bit error rate (BER) decreases much more slowly than predicted by the waterfall-region behavior [2]. Minimum distance is a key global parameter in this regime: a small min-imum distance implies the existence of low-weight codewords that can dominate high-SNR performance, especially under maximum-likelihood or near-maximum-likelihood decoding. Under iterative BP decoding, small trapping or absorbing structures also play a central role. For this reason, computing and bounding the minimum distance of LDPC codes remains a basic problem in coding theory and code design [3]–[5]. In the corresponding incidence-hypergraph representation, variables become hyperedges and parity checks become vertices; codewords then correspond to 

even covers , namely subfamilies of hyperedges in which every vertex has an even degree [6], [7]. These equivalent viewpoints connect minimum distance, even covers, and trapping sets through the same parity constraint. The unrestricted MDP is known to be computationally hard. Classical results establish the NP-completeness of maximum-likelihood decoding and related weight problems [8], the NP-completeness of minimum distance computation [5], and strong inapproximability results for minimum distance [9]. For trapping set-related structures, Dehghan and Banihashemi [10], [11] established hardness results for locating elementary trapping sets and small cycles. In parameterized complexity, Bhattacharyya et al. [12] showed W[1]-hardness for the param-eterized minimum distance /even cover problem. What remains less well understood is how this hardness behaves under the uniform-degree structures that arise naturally in LDPC Tanner graphs, such as left regular and ( J, K)-regular configurations. In this paper, we systematically study the hardness of computing the minimum distance in left regular and biregular LDPC Tanner graphs. We prove that, for every fixed J ≥ 3, the problem is NP-complete and W[1]-complete on J-left regular Tanner graphs. We also prove NP-completeness and W[1]-completeness for (3 , 3)-regular bipartite graphs, and W[1]-completeness for ( J, K)-regular graphs for every fixed J, K ≥ 3. The reductions rely on a degree-preserving transformation framework based on hyperedge decomposition, check node splitting, and controlled variable replication. Each transforma-tion is designed to preserve an explicit bijection between fea-sible parity structures so that minimum-cardinality solutions can be tracked across di ff erent degree distributions. Overall, the paper shows that natural regularity constraints do not, by themselves, remove the intrinsic di ffi culty of exact minimum distance computation. By presenting the minimum distance, even-cover, and trapping-set formulations in a unified framework, the results clarify the complexity boundary for regular LDPC code analysis and provide guidance on where exact algorithms are unlikely to be practical. A. Graphs and Hypergraphs 

A graph is a pair G = (V, E), where V is a set of vertices and E is a set of edges. The graph is undirected if each edge connects two vertices without orientation. A graph is 

simple if it has no loops and no multiple edges. Thus, for a simple undirected graph, each edge can be written as an unordered pair {u, v} with u, v ∈ V and u , v. Unless otherwise specified, all graphs referred to in the following text are simple undirected graphs. Two vertices u, v ∈ V(G) are said to be 

adjacent in G if {u, v} ∈ E(G). In this case, we also say that 

u is adjacent to v, or that u is a neighbor of v. For a vertex 

v ∈ V(G), its degree in G, denoted by deg G(v), is the number of vertices adjacent to v. A graph G is called k-regular if deg G(v) = k for every v ∈ V(G). For a graph G = (V, E) and a subset U ⊆ V, the induced subgraph of G on U, denoted by G[U], is the graph with vertex set U and edge set 

E(G[U]) = {{ u, v} ∈ E : u, v ∈ U}.

A path of length ℓ in G is a sequence of distinct vertices 

v0, v1, . . . , vℓ

such that {vi−1, vi} ∈ E for every 1 ≤ i ≤ ℓ. A cycle of length 

ℓ is a sequence 

v0, v1, . . . , vℓ−1, v0,

where v0, v1, . . . , vℓ−1 are distinct, ℓ ≥ 3, and 

{vi−1, vi} ∈ E for 1 ≤ i ≤ ℓ − 1, {vℓ−1, v0} ∈ E.

Equivalently, a cycle is obtained from a path by adding an edge between its two endpoints. The girth of G, denoted by 

g(G), is the length of the shortest cycle in G. If G contains no cycle, then its girth is defined to be infinite. We shall also use hypergraphs. A hypergraph generalizes an ordinary graph: in an ordinary graph, each edge is incident to exactly two vertices, whereas in a hypergraph, a hyperedge may be incident to any positive number of vertices. 

Definition 1 (Hypergraph) . A hypergraph is denoted by 

H = (V, E), E = (eα)α∈I ,

where V is a finite set of vertices, I is a finite index set, and each e α is a nonempty subset of V, called a hyperedge .

We use an indexed family of hyperedges rather than a set of subsets: if α , β but eα = eβ, then eα and eβ are still regarded as two distinct hyperedges. This convention is useful for representing multiple hyperedges. A vertex v ∈ V is said to be incident with a hyperedge eα

The hypergraph is J-uniform if every hyperedge has cardinality 

J, that is, |eα| = J for every α ∈ I. It is K-regular if every vertex is incident with exactly K hyperedges, that is, deg H (v) = K for every v ∈ V. It is linear if any two distinct indexed hyperedges intersect in at most one vertex: 

|eα ∩ eβ| ≤ 1, ∀α , β.

D. Complexity-Theoretic Background 

NP -completeness: The class NP consists of decision prob-lems for which a “yes” instance admits a certificate verifiable in polynomial time. A problem is NP-complete if it belongs to NP and every problem in NP is polynomial-time reducible to it [14]. 

Parameterized complexity: Parameterized complexity stud-ies decision problems together with an auxiliary parame-ter [15]. A parameterized problem is a language L ⊆ Σ∗ × N,whose instances are pairs ( x, k), where k is the parameter. 

Definition 7 (Fixed-parameter tractability) . A parameterized problem L is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT ) if there exists an algorithm deciding whether (x, k) ∈ L in time f (k)|x|O(1) ,where f is a computable function depending only on k. 

The class FPT contains all fixed-parameter tractable prob-lems. The W-hierarchy provides a standard framework for identifying parameterized problems that are unlikely to be fixed-parameter tractable [16]. 

Definition 8 (W[1]-completeness) . A parameterized problem is W[1] -hard if every problem in W[1] admits an FPT -reduction to it. It is W[1] -complete if it is W[1] -hard and also belongs to W[1] .

An FPT-reduction from a parameterized problem L1 to another parameterized problem L2 maps an instance ( x, k) to an instance ( x′, k′) in time f (k)|x|O(1) such that ( x, k) ∈ L1 if and only if ( x′, k′) ∈ L2, and k′ ≤ g(k) for some computable function g [17].

Remark 10 (Parameterized equivalence) . Within each struc-tural class—unrestricted, column-regular, and biregular—the matrix, Tanner graph, and hypergraph formulations are equiv-alent under parameter-preserving transformations. Columns of H correspond to variable nodes in the Tanner graph and to hyperedges in the hypergraph, whereas rows of H correspond to check nodes and to hypergraph vertices. These transforma-tions are computable in linear time, preserve the target size w, and maintain the relevant uniformity or regularity restrictions. Therefore, the reductions below may be stated in whichever of the three equivalent languages is most convenient. 

IV. I ntermediate Transformations 

In this section, we present three transformation primitives that are used repeatedly in the reductions, especially in the proofs in Section V and Section VI. The main di ffi culty in proving hardness for structured LDPC codes is transforming arbitrary instances into regular or nearly regular configurations without changing the underlying parity problem. In particular, the transformations must avoid creating artificial low-weight solutions and must preserve the correspondence between feasible supports in the original and transformed instances. The three primitives developed below —hyperedge decomposition , check node splitting , and 

variable replication —provide the tools needed to control degrees while preserving the solution space through explicit maps. We begin by addressing a fundamental obstacle that arises in reductions involving hypergraph representations: the presence of hyperedges with arbitrarily large cardinality. In the context of MDPs for regular LDPC codes, we often need to work with 3-uniform hypergraphs (corresponding to variable nodes of degree three in the Tanner graph). However, general in-stances may contain hyperedges involving many vertices. The following decomposition procedure resolves this mismatch by systematically breaking down large hyperedges into chains of size-three components while introducing auxiliary vertices to maintain structural integrity.

The three algorithmic primitives developed in this section form an integrated framework underpinning all subsequent hardness reductions. Each transformation addresses a distinct structural challenge while adhering to a unifying principle: preserving bijective correspondences between solution spaces. Lemma 11 enforces hyperedge cardinality constraints without altering even cover structure; Lemma 12 reduces check node degrees to three while maintaining trapping set equivalence; and Lemma 13 amplifies variable contributions compatibly with strict regularity requirements. In Section V, we leverage Algorithm 1 to transform arbitrary hypergraphs into 3-uniform configurations, then employ Algorithm 3 to preserve minimum even covers. The proof of Section VI incorporates Algorithm 2 to achieve full (3 , 3)-regularity, followed by variable replica-tion operations enforcing degree bounds on both bipartition sides. All three algorithms run in polynomial time and are lo-cal : each transformation operates independently on individ-ual structural elements without requiring global coordination. This makes the transformations easy to compose in the later reductions. The auxiliary structures introduced—chains of sub-hyperedges, split check node sequences, and variable replica-tion clusters—exhibit predictable properties facilitating rigor-ous analysis of solution cardinalities in transformed instances. While presented in the context of minimum distance and trapping set problems, this degree-preserving transformation framework extends to broader combinatorial problems on graphs and hypergraphs where parity constraints are central, and may find independent utility in other hardness reductions involving structured constraint satisfaction or coding-theoretic constructions. V. L eft Regular Minimum Distance Problem 

We first study the MDP for Tanner graphs with constant left degree, equivalently parity-check matrices with constant column weight. By the codeword—even cover correspondence in Proposition 6, this is the same as studying minimum even covers in uniform hypergraphs. The cases J ≤ 2 are polynomial-time tractable and therefore do not exhibit the hardness phenomena considered here. When 

J = 1, the associated hypergraph is 1-uniform, so the problem reduces to checking whether some vertex is incident with two identical singleton hyperedges; in the simple-hypergraph case, no nonempty even cover exists. When J = 2, the associated hypergraph is an ordinary graph, possibly with parallel edges: hyperedges become graph edges, and nonempty even covers are exactly nonempty unions of cycles. The minimum distance is therefore the length of a shortest cycle, with a pair of parallel edges counted as a cycle of length two. This can be found in polynomial time. Thus J = 3 is the first left-degree regime in which computational hardness can arise. We show that this threshold is tight. The proof for J =

3 reduces the unrestricted minimum even cover problem to the 3-uniform case. The reduction combines hyperedge decomposition, padding, and controlled variable replication. The maps used in this proof are explicitly built from the bijections established in the intermediate transformations: the hyperedge-decomposition bijection T constructed in the proof of Lemma 11, and the variable-replication bijections Fv,m

constructed in the proof of Lemma 13. We then extend the result to every fixed J > 3 by adding global check nodes adjacent to all variables. Since every trapping set in a 3-left regular Tanner graph has even cardinality, these global checks are automatically satisfied and do not change the feasible supports.

Together, Theorems 14 and 15 establish a sharp complexity boundary for left regular Tanner graphs. Column weights at most two admit polynomial-time algorithms, whereas every fixed column weight J ≥ 3 already yields NP-complete and W[1]-complete instances. Thus left regularity alone does not mitigate the intrinsic di ffi culty of minimum distance computation. This motivates the biregular setting considered next, where both variable node and check node degrees are simultaneously constrained. VI. B iregular Minimum Distance Problem 

We now impose regularity on both sides of the Tanner graph. In matrix terms, this corresponds to parity-check matrices with constant column weight and constant row weight; in the hypergraph formulation, it corresponds to uniform regular incidence structures. This setting is central for LDPC codes since many standard ensembles and explicit constructions use fixed variable node and check node degrees to control local graph structure and iterative-decoding behavior. The low-degree regimes remain algorithmically benign. If every check node has degree one, each parity check forces its unique neighboring variable to be zero. If every check node has degree two, each parity check imposes an equality constraint between two variables, and the instance reduces to a graph of equality components. In that case, the minimum nonzero support can be found by identifying the smallest feasible component. Therefore, the first right-degree regime in which the hardness phenomena considered here can occur is K = 3. The goal of this section is to show that full biregularity does not restore tractability once both degrees are at least three. We first prove hardness for the base case (3 , 3). The proof starts from the 3-left regular case, uses the check node splitting map 

Q constructed in the proof of Lemma 12, and then applies the replication maps Fv,m constructed in the proof of Lemma 13 to control the cardinality of auxiliary solutions. We then extend the parameterized hardness to arbitrary fixed degrees ( J, K)with J, K ≥ 3: the right degree is increased through high-girth auxiliary structures, and the left degree is increased by a replica-and-global-check construction. 

A. Intractability of the (3 , 3) -Regular Case 

The base case (3 , 3) requires simultaneous control of the two sides of the bipartition. Starting from the hardness of the 3-left regular case, we first reduce all check node degrees to at most three. These degree imbalances are then repaired by duplicating the instance, coupling corresponding degree-two variables, and amplifying selected non-auxiliary variables so that no smaller trapping set can be supported primarily on the auxiliary structure. The number of copies and the length of the iterative construction are chosen so that every trapping set using the newly introduced su ffi x variables is necessarily larger than the relevant threshold. Therefore, minimum trapping sets in the final (3 , 3)-regular instance correspond to minimum trapping sets in the original instance.

Thus even highly structured regular LDPC Tanner graphs retain the intrinsic combinatorial hardness of finding low-weight nonzero codewords. VII. D iscussion 

The intractability results established in this work have direct implications for the design and analysis of LDPC codes. Since minimum distance computation remains hard even under strong regularity constraints, practical code design cannot rely on exact minimum distance evaluation except for highly restricted families. A more viable approach is to combine heuristic search, provable lower bounds, and additional alge-braic or combinatorial structure. In particular, regular LDPC codes arising from finite geometries, incidence designs, or other highly symmetric constructions may contain exploitable features that are absent from arbitrary regular Tanner graphs. These results are also relevant to the error-floor phenomenon in iterative LDPC decoding. Low-weight codewords and small even-parity structures are among the principal contributors to high-SNR error floors. The hardness results proved here indicate that exhaustive identification of such structures is computationally prohibitive in general regular ensembles. Consequently, e ff ective error-floor analysis should emphasize structure-aware enumeration, probabilistic prediction, and mit-igation mechanisms that avoid solving the exact MDP as a subroutine. A central open direction is to determine whether the bireg-ular hardness results can be strengthened from parameterized hardness to full NP-completeness in the linear-hypergraph setting. This work provides a complexity-theoretic characterization of the MDP for regular LDPC codes. The main results are as follows: (i) For every fixed J ≥ 3, the MDP for J-left regular Tanner graphs is NP-complete and W[1]-complete; (ii) For (3 , K)-regular bipartite graphs with every fixed K ≥ 3, the problem is NP-complete and W[1]-complete; (iii) For ( J, K)-regular instances with every fixed J, K ≥ 3, the problem is W[1]-complete. The reductions are organized around a degree-preserving transformation framework consisting of hyperedge decompo-sition, check node splitting, and controlled variable replication. The key technical point is not merely that these transforma-tions preserve feasibility, but that their proofs construct explicit maps between solution spaces: the even-cover bijection T in Lemma 11, the trapping-set bijection Q in Lemma 12, and the replication bijections Fv,m in Lemma 13. These maps allow the subsequent reductions to track feasible supports and minimum-cardinality solutions through increasingly constrained graph classes. By unifying minimum distance, even covers, and trapping sets within a single parity-based framework, the paper clarifies why exact minimum distance computation remains in-trinsically di ffi cult for regular LDPC codes and why structural restrictions alone are insu ffi cient to eliminate this hardness.
